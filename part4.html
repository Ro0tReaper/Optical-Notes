Section 01

What Is an Access Network?

Think of the internet as a highway system. The backbone network is the wide multi-lane motorway between cities. An access network is the neighbourhood street that connects your house to that motorway. Without it, none of the high-speed backbone capacity reaches you.

The Core Definition

The access network — also called the "first-mile" network (or last-mile, depending on direction of view) — connects service provider Central Offices (COs) to individual homes and businesses. It is the bridge between you and the internet.

Other names you will see in literature: last-mile network, subscriber access network, local loop.

Why Does Everyone Care?

Two groups have very different but equally pressing demands:

  • Residential subscribers want high bandwidth for streaming video, gaming, and media-rich internet — and they want it at a price comparable to what they already pay.
  • Corporate / business users want broadband infrastructure to connect their local-area networks (LANs) to the internet backbone reliably and at speed.
  • Over recent years, there has been enormous interest in developing high-capacity access networks to satisfy both groups.
Section 02

Current Access Network Solutions

Before optical technologies took over, two technologies dominated broadband access. Both are essentially legacy infrastructure repurposed for data — which is precisely where their problems come from.

DSL — Digital Subscriber Line

DSL piggybacks data on top of the existing copper telephone wire already going into homes. It was never designed for high-speed data — the phone line infrastructure was built purely to carry voice.

  • Distance limit: A DSL subscriber must be within 18,000 feet (~5.5 km) of the Central Office, because copper signal degrades over distance.
  • Retrofitted version of voice infrastructure — not optimal for data.

VDSL — Very High Bit-Rate DSL

A newer variant of DSL that dramatically increases speed but at the cost of range.

  • Can support up to 50 Mbps downstream bandwidth.
  • Severe distance limitation: works only up to 1,500 feet (~460 m) from the CO — about 12× shorter than regular DSL.

CATV — Community Antenna Television (Cable TV)

CATV delivers internet by dedicating some Radio Frequency (RF) channels inside co-axial cable (the same cable that carries broadcast TV) for data traffic.

  • Mainly built for broadcast — all subscribers receive the same signal, which doesn't fit well for distributing individual access bandwidth.
  • Network performance degrades at high load because all subscribers share the same cable segment.
Key Insight

Both DSL and CATV are built on infrastructure meant for voice and analogue TV respectively. Their data capabilities are retrofits — clever, but fundamentally not optimal for modern broadband demands.

Section 03

The Bandwidth Bottleneck Challenge

Here is the brutal gap that defines why access networks are a crisis:

Network Segment Technology Bandwidth
Backbone (city-to-city) OC-192 optical links 10 Gbps
Access (last mile, DSL) DSL (current-gen) 1.5 Mbps downstream / 128 Kbps upstream
The Bottleneck

The backbone network is delivering 10 Gbps, but the access network hands only 1.5 Mbps to each subscriber. Imagine a wide 10-lane motorway suddenly narrowing to a single dirt track before reaching your house. The access network is the bottleneck for broadband services.

Users demand bandwidth for video-on-demand, interactive gaming, and video conferencing — applications that old DSL simply cannot support at scale. A solution that matches backbone capacity all the way to the user's home is clearly needed.

Section 04

Future Access Networks — Enter PON

The answer to the bottleneck is bringing optical fibre as close to the end-user as possible. This is the FTTx (Fibre-To-The-x) family of models, centred around the Passive Optical Network (PON) architecture.

FTTx — Fibre to the…

  • FTTH — Fibre to the Home: fibre runs all the way into your house.
  • FTTB — Fibre to the Building: fibre enters the building, then copper/ethernet to each unit.
  • FTTC — Fibre to the Curb: fibre ends at a street cabinet near your home; short copper run to the house.

All of these offer unprecedented access bandwidth compared to legacy solutions.

Visual Comparison (From the Lecture Chart)

A graph of line rate vs. reach distance clearly shows: PON achieves Gbps speeds at distances up to 20 km — far beyond what DSL, VDSL, ISDN, or dial-up can manage at any useful distance. PON is in a completely different league.

Section 05

FTTH Deployment Scenarios

There are three ways to design an FTTH network. The lecture presents them in order of increasing efficiency. To compare them, use a standard setup: N subscribers, each at an average distance of L km from the Central Office.

1

Point-to-Point (PtP)

A dedicated fibre runs from the CO directly to each subscriber — like each house having its own private motorway on-ramp.

  • Each subscriber gets a dedicated connection — full bandwidth, full privacy.
  • Very simple to understand and manage.
Transceivers 2N Fibre Length N × L km

⚠ Very expensive at scale — N fibres must be run from CO to N homes.

2

Curb-Switched

One fibre runs from CO to a street-level active switch (the "curb switch"), which then branches out to each home. Like a distribution centre.

  • Fibre between CO and curb switch is shared — saves cable.
  • Requires electrical power and backup power at the curb switch.
Transceivers 2N + 2 Fibre Length ≈ L km

⚠ Active electronics in the field = power requirements + maintenance burden.

3

PON (Passive Splitter)

One fibre from CO to a passive optical splitter in the field, which splits the signal to all subscribers. No electricity needed at the splitter.

  • Minimises fibre, transceivers, and CO terminations.
  • No power or maintenance at the remote node.
Transceivers N + 1 Fibre Length L km

✓ Most cost-effective and widely used in real deployments.

Exam Tip — The Numbers

Remember the transceiver counts: PtP = 2N, Curb-switched = 2N+2, PON = N+1. PON is the clear winner because N+1 is far fewer than 2N for any large N (e.g. 32 subscribers → PON needs 33 transceivers vs PtP needing 64).

Section 06

PON Technologies — Deep Dive

What Exactly Is a PON?

A Passive Optical Network (PON) is a point-to-multipoint (PtMP) optical network in which there are no active (powered) elements in the signal path between source and destination. All splitting/combining is done by passive optical components. This is its killer advantage — no field electronics to power, maintain, or replace.

Optical Splitters & Couplers

The magic of a PON is in the passive splitter. Here is how it works:

  • A PON uses couplers (passive devices) to split an optical signal from one fibre into several, and combine signals from multiple fibres into one — all with no electricity.
  • An optical coupler is simply two fibres fused together. Light entering any input port is automatically split between both output ports.
  • Using 1:2 tap couplers and 1:N splitters, a PON can be deployed in tree, tree-and-branch, ring, or bus topologies.

OLT and ONUs — The Two Players

All communication in a PON is between exactly two types of equipment:

DeviceFull NameWhereRole
OLT Optical Line Terminal Inside the CO Connects optical access network to the MAN / backbone / long-haul network
ONU Optical Network Unit At end-user (FTTH/FTTB) or at the curb (FTTC) Terminates the optical signal and delivers service to the subscriber

Advantages of PON

  • Long reach: operates over distances up to 20 km between CO and customer premises.
  • Minimal fibre: minimises fibre deployment in both the CO and the local loop.
  • High bandwidth: deeper fibre penetration enables Gbps-class solutions.
  • Broadcast-capable downstream: operates as a broadcast network in the downstream direction, allowing video broadcasting to all subscribers simultaneously.

Burst Mode Transceivers — The Near-Far Problem

This is a challenging topic but important. Here is the story:

The Near-Far Problem

Because ONUs are at different distances from the OLT, their signals travel different lengths of fibre. Longer fibre = more attenuation = weaker signal arriving at the OLT. So the OLT receives signals from 32 ONUs all at different power levels.

  • If OLT is tuned for a close, strong ONU → it may read the weak signal from a distant ONU as all zeros (signal too faint).
  • If OLT is tuned for a distant, weak ONU → it may read the strong signal from a close ONU as all ones (signal too strong = saturated).

Solution: Burst-Mode Receiver at OLT

The OLT receiver must quickly re-adjust its decision threshold (the boundary between reading a bit as "0" or "1") at the beginning of each received time slot. This is called operating in burst mode. Only the OLT needs this capability — the ONUs receive a continuous downstream stream and don't need to re-adjust quickly.

Alternative Solution: ONU Power Adjustment

ONUs can be instructed to adjust their transmitter power so all signals arrive at the OLT at equal power. However:

  • Makes ONU hardware more complex and expensive.
  • Requires a special feedback signalling protocol from OLT back to each ONU.

The Capture Effect — Another Problem

Even when an ONU is not transmitting, its laser produces spontaneous emission noise. Noise from multiple nearby ONUs can easily drown out the weak signal from a distant ONU — this is called the capture effect.

  • Fix: ONUs must shut down their lasers between assigned time slots.
  • But: when a laser is turned back on, it needs time to warm up and stabilise — its power may fluctuate at the start. Solution: use fast-stabilising lasers that reach stable output very quickly.
Section 07

EPON — Ethernet Passive Optical Network

EPON (Ethernet PON) carries all traffic encapsulated in standard Ethernet frames, as defined in the IEEE 802.3 standard. It operates at standard Ethernet data rates — making it easy to integrate with existing Ethernet-based enterprise and home networks.

Think of EPON as a PON wearing an Ethernet costume — your data is packaged the same way as it would be on your home Wi-Fi router, just travelling over optical fibre.

Downstream Direction (OLT → ONUs)

This is the easy, broadcast direction — like a TV tower sending to all receivers:

  • Ethernet frames from the OLT pass through the 1:N passive splitter and physically reach every ONU.
  • Typical values of N (split ratio) are between 8 and 32.
  • Since every ONU receives every frame, each ONU is assigned a Logical Link Identifier (LLID) when it registers with the network.
  • Each ONU only processes (keeps) frames carrying its own LLID — it ignores (discards) all others.

Upstream Direction (ONUs → OLT)

This direction is trickier because the optical splitter is used in reverse as a combiner:

  • Due to the directional properties of a passive optical combiner, data frames from any ONU travel only toward the OLT — they cannot reach other ONUs. This is a natural privacy benefit.
  • In this sense, the upstream direction behaves like a point-to-point architecture between each ONU and the OLT.
  • Collision problem: if two ONUs transmit simultaneously, their frames arrive at the OLT at the same time and collide (corrupt each other).
Solution: Arbitration / TDMA

ONUs must use an arbitration mechanism — typically Time Division Multiple Access (TDMA) — where the OLT assigns each ONU specific time windows to transmit. No two ONUs transmit at the same time, so no collisions occur. This is similar to how a teacher controls which student speaks — one at a time.

Section 08

WDM-PON — Wavelength Division Multiplexing PON

Basic PON uses a single optical channel (wavelength) — its bandwidth is therefore limited. WDM-PON extends this by using multiple wavelengths simultaneously on the same fibre, dramatically multiplying capacity.

The Core Idea

A WDM-PON assigns a separate dedicated wavelength between the OLT and each ONU. Think of it like each subscriber having their own private colour of light — their data travels on their colour and nobody else's.

This transforms the network from point-to-multipoint (basic PON) into effectively a set of virtual point-to-point links, one per subscriber.

Advantages of WDM-PON over Basic PON

  • Different ONUs can be served at different bit rates if needed (flexibility).
  • WDM-PON does not suffer power-splitting losses — since each ONU gets its own wavelength, the signal isn't split/attenuated by sharing.

WDM-PON Architectures — Design Challenge

All WDM-PON designs use a separate downstream wavelength per ONU. The architectures differ mainly in how the upstream direction is handled. There are two reasons upstream is harder:

  • ONU equipment (especially transmitters) must be inexpensive.
  • It is preferable to avoid wavelength-specific hardware at the ONU — if the ONU is locked to a specific wavelength, it cannot be easily replaced or reused elsewhere (business/operational reason).
Key Hardware: AWG

Most WDM-PON designs use an Arrayed Waveguide Grating (AWG) — a passive device that routes different wavelengths to different ports. An AWG acts like a prism for light inside a chip: input wavelengths are separated and directed to their respective ONUs (downstream), or combined (upstream).

The Four WDM-PON Architectures

① C-PON — Composite PON

Downstream: Uses WDM in the 1550-nm band — a separate wavelength for each ONU from an AWG-based passive router.

Upstream: Uses a single shared wavelength in the 1300-nm band — all ONUs share it through TDMA (Time Division Multiple Access). Only one ONU transmits at a time.

How both directions fit on one fibre: By using two very different wavelength bands (1550 nm and 1300 nm), upstream and downstream can coexist on the same single fibre through CWDM (Coarse WDM).

Special requirement: The OLT has a single-wavelength burst-mode receiver that can synchronise to the clock signals from different ONUs (which are at different distances and therefore arrive at slightly different timings).

② LARNET — Local Access Remote Network

In LARNET, each ONU has a standard receiver for downstream (receiving its dedicated wavelength from the AWG) and an LED for upstream transmission.

Why an LED? LEDs are cheap and produce broadband (wide-spectrum) light. The AWG at the remote node acts as a filter — it accepts light from an LED at the ONU and routes it to the correct upstream wavelength toward the OLT.

At the OLT side, a single broadband receiver handles all upstream wavelengths. This architecture keeps ONU costs very low.

③ RITENET — Remote Integrated Terminal Network

RITENET takes an elegant approach to the "don't put wavelength-specific transmitters at the ONU" problem: the OLT sends the light; the ONU just modulates it.

How it works:

  • The OLT has a tunable laser / WDM source and sends unmodulated CW (continuous-wave) light at a specific wavelength to each ONU via a 2×2N AWG-based router.
  • The ONU has a receiver (for downstream data) and a modulator (to imprint upstream data onto the same wavelength the OLT sent).
  • The modulated light travels back through the AWG to the OLT's tunable receiver / WDM receiver.

Key benefit: ONUs have no lasers — only cheap modulators — so there is no wavelength-specific active source at the ONU. The OLT controls everything.

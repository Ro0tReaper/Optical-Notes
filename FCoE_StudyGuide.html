Lesson 1

What is FCoE? & Why It Exists

🧩 Big Picture Analogy Analogy
Imagine a data center as an office building. Right now it has two completely separate cable systems running through the walls: one set of cables for internet/LAN traffic (Ethernet) and another set of cables for storage (FC). Every server needs two network cards, two cable runs, two switch types. That's double the cost, double the power, double the complexity. FCoE is like building one single unified cable system that carries both types of traffic simultaneously — the storage data rides inside the Ethernet cable, hidden in a special compartment. You rip out the duplicate infrastructure and replace it with one converged network.
⚙️ Technical Definition Technical

FCoE (Fibre Channel over Ethernet) is a SAN technology that transports FC storage traffic alongside regular Ethernet traffic over a single Converged Enhanced Ethernet (CEE) network.

  • Uses the FCoE protocol to encapsulate FC frames inside Ethernet frames — the FC frame is placed inside the Ethernet frame's payload.
  • Ensures lossless delivery of FC traffic over Ethernet (regular Ethernet can drop frames; CEE adds enhancements to prevent this — covered in Lesson 2).
  • FC stays FC — the FC frame itself is unchanged. Only the outer wrapping changes to Ethernet.

Why FCoE? — The Drivers

Traditional data centers have two separate physical networks side by side: an Ethernet LAN network for compute-to-compute traffic, and a separate FC SAN for compute-to-storage traffic. That means:

  • Two types of adapters per server (NIC + FC HBA)
  • Two cable runs per server
  • Two sets of switches (Ethernet switches + FC switches)
  • More power draw, more rack space, more management complexity
FCoE solves this by converging both networks into one:
✅ Reduces the number of adapters, cables, and switches
✅ Reduces power and space consumption in the data center
✅ Simplifies management — one network instead of two
Key insight for the exam: FCoE does NOT replace FC. The FC fabric and FC protocols still exist underneath. FCoE just replaces the FC physical cables between servers and the SAN with shared Ethernet cables — saving infrastructure costs while keeping the FC architecture intact.

Lesson 1

FCoE SAN Components

FCoE SAN has three key component categories: network adapters, cables, and the FCoE switch.

1. Network Adapters

CNA — Converged Network Adapter

  • A single physical card that does the job of BOTH a NIC and an FC HBA
  • Encapsulates FC frames into Ethernet frames and sends them over CEE links
  • Has three separate ASICs (chips) on one card: 10GE ASIC (Ethernet), FC ASIC (Fibre Channel), FCoE ASIC (translation layer)
  • One card → replaces two cards → saves a PCIe slot, power, cost

Software FCoE Adapter

  • A software module running in the OS or hypervisor kernel
  • Performs FCoE processing using an existing supported NIC
  • The NIC carries both FCoE storage traffic AND regular Ethernet LAN traffic simultaneously
  • Lower cost (no extra hardware) but uses CPU cycles

2. Cables

Same as regular Ethernet — copper (for short distances) and fiber optic cables. No special FC-specific cables needed on the CEE portion of the network.

3. FCoE Switch — The Most Important Component

Think of the FCoE switch as a bilingual translator living at the border between two countries — the Ethernet world and the FC world. Packets arrive from the Ethernet side, the switch strips off the Ethernet wrapping, extracts the FC frame inside, and passes it into the FC fabric. In the other direction, FC frames come in and get wrapped in Ethernet before going out to the CNA.

The FCoE switch has three internal building blocks:

PartWhat it does
FCF (Fibre Channel Forwarder)The brain of the FCoE switch. It bridges CEE ↔ FC worlds. It handles FCoE login requests, applies zoning, provides all FC fabric services, and encapsulates/decapsulates FC frames.
Ethernet BridgeThe regular Ethernet switching logic. Handles forwarding of regular LAN traffic and FCoE frames on the Ethernet side.
PortsHas both FC ports (to connect to FC SAN or FC switches) and Ethernet ports (to connect to compute systems via CEE links)

The FCoE switch forwards frames based on Ethertype — a field in the Ethernet header that tells the switch what type of payload is inside (FCoE traffic gets a specific Ethertype value that identifies it as FCoE, so the switch knows to route it through the FCF instead of the Ethernet bridge).

FCF = Fibre Channel Forwarder. This is the key term. It's not just a switch — it's the functional component that makes FCoE possible by bridging between CEE and FC. Expect exam questions about what FCF does.

FCoE SAN Connectivity Types

Think of two deployment scenarios. The first is like a renovation: you already have FC SAN wiring in the back of the building (storage side stays FC), and you just replace the cable runs from servers to the main switch with a new converged cable. The second is a fresh build from scratch — everything is FCoE end-to-end, no legacy FC cables anywhere.

FCoE with Existing FC SAN

  • Compute systems have CNAs, connected via CEE links to FCoE switches
  • FCoE switches then connect to the existing FC SAN via standard FC links
  • Storage systems keep their existing FC ports — no changes needed on the storage side
  • Suitable for: Environments that already have an FC SAN and want to reduce server-side cabling without rebuilding storage infrastructure
  • The FCoE switch acts as the bridge: CEE on one side, FC SAN on the other

End-to-End FCoE

  • Compute systems have CNAs connected to FCoE switches via CEE links
  • Multiple FCoE switches interconnect to form the fabric
  • Storage systems also have FCoE ports — the FC SAN fabric itself runs over CEE
  • Suitable for: New deployments built entirely on FCoE from the start
  • No traditional FC cables anywhere — everything goes over CEE
FCoE with Existing FC SAN flow:
Compute System (CNA) → CEE Link → FCoE Switch (FCF converts FCoE ↔ FC) → FC SAN → Storage System (FC ports)
End-to-End FCoE flow:
Compute System (CNA) → CEE Link → FCoE Switch → CEE Link → FCoE Switch → Storage System (FCoE ports)

VLAN & VSAN in FCoE

Think of VLAN as a virtual room inside an Ethernet network — you logically separate traffic without running separate cables. VSAN is the same concept but for the FC SAN side — a virtual storage fabric. In FCoE, they must be paired: each VSAN (storage zone) gets its own dedicated VLAN (Ethernet lane) to travel through. And critically, those storage-carrying VLANs must NOT be shared with regular LAN traffic — they're reserved exclusively for storage.
  • In an FCoE with existing FC SAN environment, both VLANs (Ethernet logical segments) and VSANs (FC virtual SANs) co-exist.
  • The FCoE switch supports VSAN-to-VLAN mapping: each VSAN is assigned a dedicated VLAN.
  • VLANs that carry FCoE/VSAN traffic must NOT carry regular LAN traffic — they are reserved solely for storage to ensure performance and lossless delivery.

Lesson 2

Converged Enhanced Ethernet (CEE) — What & Why

Regular Ethernet is like a highway that can drop cars (frames) when traffic gets congested — if the exit ramp (receive buffer) is full, the car just gets discarded. For web browsing, that's OK — TCP retransmits. But for storage I/O, dropping a frame is catastrophic — it would corrupt your data operation. CEE is an upgraded version of Ethernet specifically designed to be lossless — it adds a set of intelligent traffic management tools that prevent frames from ever being dropped, no matter the congestion level.

CEE (Converged Enhanced Ethernet) = a set of enhancements added to standard Ethernet to make it:

  • Lossless — FC traffic absolutely cannot be dropped. Regular Ethernet can and does drop frames. CEE prevents this.
  • Convergence-capable — able to carry multiple traffic types (LAN, SAN, IPC) on the same physical link without any one type hurting another.

CEE requires four specific functionalities working together:

PFC
Priority-Based Flow Control

Pauses specific traffic classes independently — doesn't freeze the whole link

ETS
Enhanced Transmission Selection

Allocates guaranteed bandwidth per traffic class; shares unused bandwidth

CN
Congestion Notification

Detects congestion and signals the SOURCE to slow down before frames are dropped

DCBX
Data Center Bridging Exchange Protocol

Auto-distributes CEE config from switches to network adapters — ensures consistency

PFC — Priority-Based Flow Control

Imagine a highway with 8 lanes. Without PFC, if there's an accident in lane 3, you close ALL 8 lanes — every car stops. That's what regular Ethernet PAUSE does — it freezes the entire link. PFC is like being able to close just lane 3 while all 7 other lanes keep flowing freely. Each lane can be independently paused and resumed.

Regular Ethernet has a basic PAUSE mechanism — but it pauses the entire physical link. That means if your FC storage traffic is congested, your LAN traffic also stops. That's unacceptable in a converged network.

PFC creates 8 virtual links (lanes) on a single physical Ethernet link. Each virtual link corresponds to a priority level (0–7) or class of service. Key properties:

  • Each virtual link can be independently paused and restarted without affecting other virtual links.
  • The PAUSE mechanism operates per priority/class of service — FCoE traffic gets its own priority lane, LAN traffic gets a different lane.
  • When the receive buffer for one priority class fills up, only that specific virtual link is PAUSED — all others continue normally.
Why PFC is the foundation of CEE: Without PFC, you cannot safely run FC storage traffic over Ethernet — any congestion on the shared link would drop FC frames, which FC doesn't handle gracefully. PFC is what makes the "lossless" in CEE possible.
Receive buffer for priority X fills up → Receiver sends PAUSE frame to transmitter → Transmitter pauses ONLY virtual link for priority X → All other priorities (1,2,3,4,5,6,7) continue flowing → Buffer clears → PAUSE lifted → transmission resumes

ETS — Enhanced Transmission Selection

Think of shared office space in a building. ETS is like a fair scheduling system for meeting rooms. LAN traffic gets rooms Mon/Wed, SAN traffic gets rooms Tue/Thu, IPC gets Fri. But if LAN doesn't need its rooms on Wednesday, ETS redistributes those unused timeslots to whoever needs them — no wasted capacity.

ETS (Enhanced Transmission Selection) manages how bandwidth is allocated across different traffic classes on the shared CEE link.

  • Allocates a guaranteed minimum bandwidth percentage to each traffic class — for example: LAN 30%, SAN/FCoE 50%, IPC 20%.
  • If one traffic class doesn't use its allocated bandwidth (e.g., LAN is quiet), ETS makes that unused bandwidth available to other classes that need more — dynamically and automatically.
  • Traffic classes include: LAN (regular Ethernet), SAN (FCoE storage), and IPC (Inter Process Communication — cluster/VM traffic).
ETS + PFC together: PFC prevents drops (lossless); ETS prevents starvation (fairness). PFC = the emergency brake. ETS = the traffic scheduler. Both are needed for a converged network where FC and LAN traffic coexist.

CN — Congestion Notification

Imagine GPS navigation sending a real-time traffic alert back through the network to every car heading toward a jam — "slow down now, there's congestion at exit 42." The cars rate-limit themselves before they even reach the bottleneck. Congestion Notification does exactly this for FCoE switches: it detects a bottleneck and sends a message back to the original sender to slow down, preventing the congestion from spreading.

CN (Congestion Notification) provides a proactive mechanism for detecting network congestion and signaling the source before frames are dropped.

Traffic builds up at a switch (congestion point) → Switch detects congestion → Switch sends Congestion Notification Message (CNM) back to the source node (Node A) → Source receives CNM → Source applies rate limiting — slows its transmission → Congestion clears → Source resumes full rate
CN is end-to-end. The slide diagram shows the CNM travelling back all the way from the congested switch to the compute system (Node A), not just to the immediately adjacent switch. The source itself rate-limits to protect the network from packet loss.

Lesson 2

DCBX — Data Center Bridging Exchange Protocol

Think of DCBX as automatic configuration sync — like how your phone automatically pulls settings from a company MDM system the moment you connect to corporate Wi-Fi. The FCoE switch (MDM) pushes all its CEE configuration (PFC settings, ETS bandwidth allocations) to every CNA and network adapter that connects to it. Every device ends up with consistent settings automatically, without manual configuration.

DCBX (Data Center Bridging Exchange Protocol) allows CEE-capable devices to discover each other and exchange/configure their CEE feature settings.

  • A switch can distribute its configuration values (PFC priorities, ETS allocations, etc.) to all attached network adapters automatically.
  • This ensures consistent CEE configuration across the entire network — no manual per-device setup needed.
  • Without DCBX, you'd have to manually configure every CNA and switch to agree on which priorities are lossless, which bandwidth percentages to use, etc. DCBX automates this.
DCBX is the glue that makes PFC + ETS + CN work consistently across all devices in the network. Think of it as the automatic "handshake and agreement" protocol — devices connect, exchange DCBX messages, agree on settings, and the CEE network becomes self-configuring.

FCoE Frame Mapping (Protocol Stack)

Think of FCoE as two existing protocol stacks being fused together. FC has its own 5-layer stack (FC-0 to FC-4). Ethernet (IEEE 802.3) has a 2-layer stack (Physical + MAC). FCoE replaces FC's physical layers (FC-0 and FC-1) with Ethernet's physical layers — the rest of FC stays exactly the same above that.
FCoE Protocol StackWhat it maps to
FC-4 (Protocol Mappings — SCSI, etc.)FC upper layers — unchanged from pure FC
FC-3 (Services)FC services — unchanged
FC-2 (Framing, addressing, flow control)FC framing — unchanged
FCoE Mapping LayerThe translation/encapsulation layer — maps FC frames into Ethernet frames. This is what FCoE adds.
Layer 2 — MAC (IEEE 802.3)Standard Ethernet MAC layer — replaces FC-1
Layer 1 — Physical (IEEE 802.3)Standard Ethernet physical — replaces FC-0
The big insight: FCoE replaces FC-0 (physical) and FC-1 (encoding) with standard Ethernet physical and MAC layers. Everything above — FC-2 (frames, addressing, flow control), FC-3, FC-4 (SCSI) — stays 100% intact. This is why an FC SAN on the other end doesn't care whether the frame came over a pure FC link or an FCoE link — the FC frame it receives is identical.

FCoE Process — 3 Phases

Think of connecting to a new company Wi-Fi for the first time. Phase 1 (Discovery): your device scans for available networks and finds the corporate access points. Phase 2 (Login): you authenticate, get an IP address, get a username confirmed. Phase 3 (Data Transfer): you're connected and actively using the network — downloading, uploading, working normally.

The FCoE process has three sequential phases every time an FCoE device connects:

PhaseWhat happens
1. Discovery Phase
  • FCFs (Fibre Channel Forwarders on FCoE switches) discover each other and form the FCoE fabric
  • FCoE nodes (CNAs on compute systems) find available FCFs they can log into
  • Nodes and FCFs discover the right VN_Port ↔ VF_Port pairings — which node port will connect to which switch fabric port
2. Login Phase
  • Virtual FC links are established: VN_Port ↔ VF_Port (node to switch) and VE_Port ↔ VE_Port (switch to switch ISLs)
  • The VN_Port performs an FC login (same FLOGI process as in FC-SAN) and receives an FC address
  • The VN_Port also receives a unique MAC address for Ethernet-level frame delivery
3. Data Transfer Phase
  • VN_Ports now send and receive regular FC frames — encapsulated in Ethernet — over the CEE network
  • From this point on, FC I/O flows exactly as in a native FC SAN, just physically transported over CEE
Power on → Discovery: CNA finds available FCFs → Login: Virtual links created, FC address obtained, MAC address assigned → Data Transfer: FC frames encapsulated in Ethernet and flowing across CEE

FIP — FCoE Initialization Protocol

FIP is the "pre-game" protocol — it's not for moving storage data. It's the discovery and registration conversation that happens before any data flows. Think of it like the handshake + ID check at the door before you're allowed into the building. Once you're verified and logged in (via FIP), the door is open and you use regular FC frames for actual I/O.

FIP (FCoE Initialization Protocol) is a separate protocol used exclusively for discovering FCFs and establishing the virtual links between FCoE nodes and FCoE switches. It does NOT carry FC storage data — it only carries discovery and login parameters.

FIP Discovery and Login Sequence

StepWho sendsFrame typePurpose
1FCoE node (CNA)FIP Solicitation (multicast)Broadcast to the network: "Are there any FCFs available for me to log into?"
2Each available FCFFIP Advertisement (unicast)FCF replies directly to the node: "I'm here, and here's my info."
3FCoE nodeFIP FLOGI RequestNode sends a FLOGI (Fabric Login) to a chosen FCF — same login as FC-SAN, but carried in FIP
4FCFFIP FLOGI AcceptFCF responds with the node's assigned FC address AND MAC address for the VN_Port
After step 4, the virtual link (VN_Port ↔ VF_Port) is fully established. From here on, the node uses regular FCoE frames (FC wrapped in Ethernet) for all storage I/O — FIP's job is done. The key detail: FIP Solicitation is multicast (sent to all); FCF Advertisement is unicast (sent directly back to the specific node).

Lesson 3

FCoE Addressing — SPMA & FPMA

In FCoE, frame forwarding on the Ethernet side uses MAC addresses (just like regular Ethernet). Every VN_Port, VF_Port, and VE_Port gets a MAC address for Ethernet-level routing.

  • VF_Ports and VE_Ports get their MAC addresses from the FCoE switch itself.
  • VN_Ports (on compute systems) can get their MAC addresses in two different ways — SPMA or FPMA.

SPMA — Server-Provided MAC Address

  • The compute system provides the MAC address to its VN_Port
  • Follows standard Ethernet conventions — MAC is either burned-in to the CNA hardware or configured by the administrator
  • The server controls its own MAC identity — same as how regular NICs work

FPMA — Fabric-Provided MAC Address

  • The FCoE switch dynamically assigns the MAC address to the VN_Port during node login
  • The MAC address has two parts: 24-bit FC-MAP (fabric identifier prefix) + 24-bit FC address (the node's FC fabric address)
  • The FC address is embedded in the MAC address — making MAC ↔ FC address mapping trivial
FPMA is clever: because the MAC address contains the FC address inside it (FCMAP + FC address = 48-bit MAC), the FCoE switch can look at a frame's destination MAC and immediately know the destination FC address without needing a separate lookup table. This simplifies routing in the fabric.

FCoE Frame Forwarding

Forwarding an FCoE frame is like a two-address delivery system. The outer envelope (Ethernet frame) carries the MAC address of the next-hop switch — just enough to get to the FCoE switch. Once there, the switch opens the envelope, reads the inner address (FC address inside the FC frame), and routes the FC frame onwards into the FC SAN based on that inner address.

To forward a frame, a sending node needs to know two addresses:

  • The MAC address of the destination FCoE switch port — used for Ethernet-level delivery across the CEE network
  • The FC address of the destination node port — used inside the FC frame for FC-level routing

The FCoE frame carries both addresses simultaneously: MAC in the outer Ethernet header, FC address embedded in the inner FC frame header.

Exam Q&A — Test Yourself

🧪 FCoE Exam Questions
Q1: What is FCoE and what is the core problem it solves?
A: FCoE (Fibre Channel over Ethernet) encapsulates FC frames inside Ethernet frames and transports them over a CEE network. It solves the data center infrastructure problem of having two separate physical networks (FC SAN + Ethernet LAN) — requiring duplicate adapters, cables, and switches per server. FCoE converges both onto a single network, reducing cost, power, and space consumption.
Q2: What is a CNA and how does it differ from having a NIC + FC HBA?
A: A CNA (Converged Network Adapter) is a single physical card that provides both NIC (Ethernet) and FC HBA (Fibre Channel) functionality in one. It has three separate ASICs on one card: a 10GE ASIC, an FC ASIC, and an FCoE ASIC. It encapsulates FC frames into Ethernet frames and sends them over CEE links. Having a CNA means one adapter instead of two — saves PCIe slots, cables, cost, and power.
Q3: What is FCF and what does it do inside an FCoE switch?
A: FCF stands for Fibre Channel Forwarder. It is the module inside an FCoE switch that acts as the communication bridge between the CEE (Ethernet) network and the FC SAN. The FCF handles FCoE login requests, applies zoning, provides all FC fabric services, and encapsulates/decapsulates FC frames as they cross between the two worlds. The FCoE switch also contains an Ethernet Bridge for regular LAN traffic.
Q4: What are the two FCoE SAN connectivity types and when is each used?
A: (1) FCoE with Existing FC SAN — compute systems with CNAs connect via CEE to FCoE switches, which then connect to a traditional FC SAN where storage systems remain on FC ports. Used when an existing FC SAN is already in place and you want to converge server-side cabling without replacing storage infrastructure. (2) End-to-End FCoE — everything is FCoE including the storage systems (which have FCoE ports). FCoE switches interconnect throughout. Used for brand-new deployments built entirely on FCoE.
Q5: Why does FCoE need CEE? Why can't it just run on regular Ethernet?
A: Regular Ethernet is a "lossy" network — when buffers fill up due to congestion, frames are simply dropped. TCP traffic recovers from this through retransmission, but FC storage traffic cannot tolerate dropped frames — it would corrupt I/O operations. CEE adds the enhancements (PFC, ETS, CN, DCBX) that make Ethernet lossless, allowing FC frames to be transported without ever being dropped regardless of congestion levels.
Q6: Explain the four CEE functionalities and what each one does.
A: PFC — creates 8 virtual links on one physical link; each can be independently paused by priority class, so FC storage traffic can be paused without freezing LAN traffic. ETS — guarantees minimum bandwidth to each traffic class (LAN, SAN, IPC) and dynamically shares unused bandwidth. CN — when a switch detects congestion, it sends a Congestion Notification Message back to the source, which then rate-limits itself before frames are dropped. DCBX — the switch automatically distributes its CEE configuration to all attached network adapters, ensuring consistent settings across the network without manual per-device configuration.
Q7: How many virtual links does PFC create, and what is the key advantage over regular Ethernet PAUSE?
A: PFC creates 8 virtual links on a single physical Ethernet link. Regular Ethernet PAUSE pauses the entire physical link — all traffic classes stop. PFC allows each virtual link to be paused and restarted independently based on user priority/class of service. So if FC traffic needs to be paused (receive buffer filling up), only the FC priority lane stops — LAN traffic in other priority lanes continues flowing normally.
Q8: What is the FCoE frame structure? What's inside an FCoE frame?
A: An FCoE frame is an Ethernet frame containing an FCoE PDU as its payload. Structure from outside in: Ethernet Header (Destination MAC, Source MAC, VLAN Tag, Ethertype) → FCoE Header (Version, Reserved) → SOF → Encapsulated FC Frame (complete and unchanged, including its own FC CRC) → EOF → FCS (Ethernet Frame Check Sequence). The FC frame inside is not modified — it is the same frame that would travel on a pure FC network.
Q9: What are the three phases of the FCoE process?
A: (1) Discovery Phase — FCFs discover each other and form the FCoE fabric; FCoE nodes find available FCFs; nodes and FCFs identify VN_Port ↔ VF_Port pairings. (2) Login Phase — virtual FC links are created (VN_Port↔VF_Port, VE_Port↔VE_Port); VN_Port performs FC login and obtains an FC address; VN_Port obtains a unique MAC address. (3) Data Transfer Phase — VN_Ports transfer FC frames (encapsulated in Ethernet) over the CEE network normally.
Q10: What is FIP and what are its four steps?
A: FIP (FCoE Initialization Protocol) is used to discover FCFs and establish virtual links before any data transfers. FIP frames carry discovery/login parameters, not FC storage data. Steps: (1) FCoE node sends multicast FIP Solicitation to find available FCFs. (2) Each FCF replies with a unicast FIP Advertisement. (3) FCoE node sends FIP FLOGI request to a chosen FCF. (4) FCF responds with FIP FLOGI Accept containing both the FC address and MAC address for the VN_Port.
Q11: What is the difference between SPMA and FPMA?
A: Both are VN_Port MAC address assignment methods. SPMA (Server-Provided MAC Address) — the compute system provides the MAC address for its VN_Port, either burned-in or admin-configured, following standard Ethernet convention. FPMA (Fabric-Provided MAC Address) — the FCoE switch dynamically assigns the MAC address to the VN_Port at login time. The FPMA MAC has two parts: a 24-bit FC-MAP (fabric identifier) + 24-bit FC address — so the FC address is embedded in the MAC itself, making routing simpler for the switch.
Q12: To forward an FCoE frame, what two addresses must a node know and why?
A: A node must know: (1) The MAC address of the destination FCoE switch port — this is used for Ethernet-level delivery across the CEE network (outer Ethernet frame header). (2) The FC address of the destination node port — this is embedded in the FC frame inside and used for FC-level routing once the FCoE switch strips off the Ethernet wrapper and forwards the FC frame into the FC SAN. Without the MAC, the Ethernet network can't deliver the frame to the right switch. Without the FC address, the switch can't route it to the right storage port.
Q13: What is VSAN-to-VLAN mapping and what rule applies to storage VLANs in FCoE?
A: In an FCoE environment with an existing FC SAN, VSANs (virtual FC fabrics) and VLANs (virtual Ethernet segments) co-exist. The FCoE switch maps each VSAN to a dedicated VLAN — each VSAN gets its own exclusive VLAN for its traffic to travel through the Ethernet network. The critical rule: VLANs that carry VSAN (FCoE) traffic must NOT also carry regular LAN traffic. They must be dedicated exclusively to storage — this maintains the lossless, predictable behavior needed for FC storage operations.
Q14: In FCoE frame mapping, what does FCoE replace in the FC protocol stack?
A: FCoE replaces FC-0 (the physical layer — cables, connectors, optical specs) and FC-1 (encoding/decoding — 8b/10b encoding) with Ethernet's physical layer (IEEE 802.3 Physical) and MAC layer (IEEE 802.3 MAC). A new FCoE Mapping Layer sits between FC-2 and the Ethernet MAC, handling encapsulation. Everything above — FC-2 (framing, addressing, flow control), FC-3, and FC-4 (SCSI) — remains completely unchanged. This is why FC SANs on the receiving end see normal FC frames.
